# Allocation of EV Charging Stations with DC Fast Chargers in Colorado Along Major Highways

The problem we address in our project is: how many new DC fast chargers should be installed in each Colorado county to maximize highway corridor coverage and meet projected EV demand, subject to the $57.1 million statewide budget?
To answer this question, we developed a county-level optimization model that integrates spatial, demographic, and infrastructure data. The model uses several key elements derived from real datasets that we found.

Based on these inputs, we estimated the number of chargers each county should have by applying spacing standards that reflect corridor traffic levels. Following state planning logic, Front Range counties (Adams, Arapahoe, Denver, Douglas, and Boulder) were assigned a higher-density requirement of one fast charger per 3 highway miles, while all other counties were assigned one charger per 10 highway miles. We then calculated demand as the difference between this required number of chargers and the number of existing DC fast ports in each county. Counties with large gaps between required and existing infrastructure were assigned higher demand scores.

We formulated an optimization model that selects the number of new DC fast chargers to install in each county. The objective is to maximize total statewide demand satisfied, ensuring the new chargers reduce county-level shortages as efficiently as possible. The model is subject to the overall NEVI budget constraint, the unit cost per station, the region fairness (each region must get at least 10% of spend) and non-negativity/integer constraints for charger allocations.

The optimization results show that the highest-priority counties for new DC fast chargers are those with long stretches of highway, modest existing infrastructure, and rapidly growing EV ownership. Many rural corridor counties: including those in the Western Slope, Southern Colorado, and Eastern Plains, show substantial gaps between required and existing charger counts. These counties receive the largest recommended allocations because they are essential for long-distance EV travel but currently underserved.

Front Range urban counties already possess relatively dense networks of DC fast chargers, so the model assigns them fewer new stations unless their highway coverage requires additional support. Overall, the results confirm that Colorado can achieve its target deployment of approximately 63 new EV charging stations with DC fast charger while remaining within the $57.1 million budget (total budget spent is $56.7 million). The optimized allocation also aligns closely with NEVI corridor goals, improving statewide connectivity along I-25, I-70, US-50, and other major corridors.

This model provides a county-level, data-driven foundation for planning DC fast-charging infrastructure that complements the state’s broader EV strategy. It highlights where investments will have the highest impact and offers a scalable framework for future expansion as EV adoption continues to increase.
